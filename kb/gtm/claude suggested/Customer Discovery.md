# Customer Discovery

Stage G0 of the [[GTM Strategy]]. Talking to real lifters and coaches before
there's anything to show them. Needs no hardware, so it can run now, in
parallel with Stage 0 physics validation — and it's the cheapest thing in
the whole project that can still change the product.

## The one rule

**Ask about the past, not the future.** People are generous and will happily
tell you they'd buy your thing. That information is worthless. What's
valuable is what they already do: what they logged last session, what app is
on their phone right now, what they stopped using and why, whether they can
find last month's numbers. This is the *Mom Test* discipline — every
question should be answerable from memory, not imagination.

Concretely, replace:

- "Would you use a sensor that tracks your lifts?" → "Walk me through how
  you tracked your last session."
- "Would you pay $200 for this?" → "What have you spent money on for
  training in the last year?"
- "Is logging annoying?" → "Show me your log. When did you last miss one?"

## Who to talk to

Roughly in order of how much can be learned per conversation:

1. **Strength coaches with athlete rosters** — the people who already buy
   VBT hardware. They know the category, have budget, feel data pain
   acutely across many athletes, and will tell you exactly why the last
   device disappointed them. Highest information density per hour.
2. **Serious individual lifters** — powerlifters, weightlifters, dedicated
   intermediate gym-goers who follow a program. The likely consumer
   segment. Ask what they log and how religiously.
3. **Physios / S&C in rehab settings** — ROM and symmetry (the two-sensor
   outputs) matter more here than load. A possible wedge if the
   weight-estimation story wobbles.
4. **Casual gym-goers** — worth a few conversations to confirm they are
   *not* the market, rather than assuming it. They'll reveal whether "I
   don't know what weight this dumbbell is" is a real felt problem.
5. **Commercial gym owners / chains** — a different motion entirely
   (facility sales). A couple of conversations to size whether it's a
   future channel, not a focus now.

## Where to find them

- Own gym, in person — the highest-quality conversations available and the
  least scalable. Start here.
- Local powerlifting / weightlifting / CrossFit clubs; meet-day volunteering
  puts you next to dozens of competitive lifters in one afternoon.
- University S&C departments and sports-science programmes — coaches there
  are used to being asked about tools, and they read papers, so the
  bar-whip physics is a real conversation opener.
- Reddit (r/powerlifting, r/weightroom, r/homegym), VBT-specific Facebook
  groups, coach Discords. Ask to talk, don't post a pitch.
- Cold email to coaches with a public presence — high response rate if the
  ask is "15 minutes about how you collect data", not "look at my product".

## What to find out

Five things, and they're the only reason for the conversation:

1. **How they track today** — app, notebook, spreadsheet, memory, nothing.
   What breaks about it.
2. **Whether weight is ever unknown or annoying to record** — this is the
   core hypothesis. Machines with no numbers, unmarked dumbbells,
   plate-math mid-session, forgetting to log. If nobody feels this, the
   differentiator is weaker than assumed.
3. **What they've bought and abandoned** — every unused fitness gadget in a
   drawer is a lesson about friction. Ask why it stopped getting used; the
   answer is usually charging, setup time, or the data going nowhere.
4. **Whether they'd tolerate hardware on the bar** — attaching something
   every set is a real cost. Listen for whether they'd rather it live in
   the gym or in their bag. (Mount specifics are TBC; don't design in the
   interview.)
5. **What decision the data would change** — if the numbers wouldn't alter
   what they do next session, the product is a toy. Push on this.

One more, free to ask and it ranks the whole of [[Channels]] for you: *where
would you have heard about something like this?* Ask everyone, tally the
answers.

## How to run it

- **15–20 minutes**, in person or on a call. Never a demo.
- **Don't describe the product until the end**, if at all. The moment you
  pitch, they start being polite and the data goes bad.
- **Record or take notes immediately after** — memory rewrites
  conversations toward what you hoped to hear.
- **~20 conversations** before drawing conclusions, weighted toward
  segments 1 and 2. Patterns start showing around 8–10; 20 is enough to
  trust them.
- **Keep a running notes file per segment** in this folder, and write the
  synthesis as a separate note when the gate is met. Log conversations as
  they happen, not in a batch at the end.

## Gate

Twenty conversations, and a written answer to: *which single segment feels
this pain most, and what are they doing about it today?* If the answer is
"nobody really" — that is a successful outcome of G0 and it saves the
Stage 3 money.
